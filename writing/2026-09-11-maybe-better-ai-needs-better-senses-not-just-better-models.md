# Maybe Better AI Needs Better Senses, Not Just Better Models

*September 11, 2026*

When people talk about making AI better, the conversation usually turns to models. We ask whether they should be larger, reason for longer, use more data, or have better architectures. This makes sense because many of the recent improvements in AI have come from exactly those directions. But I have started wondering whether some of the limitations we attribute to intelligence are actually limitations of perception.

AI systems today are very good at working with the kinds of information that computers already know how to capture. They can read text, analyze images, listen to audio, and increasingly understand video. These abilities are impressive enough that it is easy to forget how narrow this sensory world still is compared with our own.

Consider cooking. A human cook does not interact with food through vision alone. We notice the smell of garlic changing in a pan, the texture of dough under our hands, the sound of something frying differently as moisture disappears, or a flavor becoming too bitter before there is any obvious visual sign. Much of cooking depends on continuously combining signals from several senses.

Yet when we try to make an AI system understand cooking, we often give it a camera.

This creates a strange mismatch. We may ask a model to understand a physical process while withholding much of the information a human would naturally use to understand that same process. If the system fails, it is tempting to conclude that the model is not intelligent enough. But perhaps the model simply cannot sense what matters.

Recipes make this problem especially obvious. Instructions such as “cook until fragrant,” “season to taste,” or “stop when the butter smells nutty” are perfectly reasonable directions for a person. For a machine, they are much harder. The difficulty is not necessarily that the language is ambiguous. The relevant information may never enter the system in the first place.

This made me think differently about what it would mean for AI to become genuinely useful in the physical world. A sufficiently powerful model can infer a great deal from proxies. A camera may learn that a certain color usually corresponds to a certain stage of cooking, for example. But a proxy is still a proxy. If two states look similar while smelling or tasting very different, additional reasoning cannot fully recover information that was never observed.

In that sense, perception places an upper bound on reasoning. A system cannot reason directly about a signal it never receives.

Cooking is only one example. Many physical environments contain information that is easy for humans to perceive but poorly represented in today's AI systems. Smell can indicate spoilage, combustion, fermentation, contamination, or chemical changes. Touch reveals pressure, softness, viscosity, and texture. Even temperature and humidity can contain information that vision alone does not capture well.

Of course, adding more sensors does not automatically create intelligence. Raw sensory data can be noisy, difficult to interpret, and expensive to collect. A sensor reading is not the same thing as understanding what that reading means. The harder problem is connecting physical signals to useful concepts: not merely detecting that something changed, but learning whether the change means the garlic is ready, the coffee is over-extracted, or the food is beginning to spoil.

Still, I find this direction interesting because it changes where we look for progress. Instead of assuming that every failure requires a smarter model, we can ask whether the model is receiving the right information in the first place.

There is also something slightly circular about the way AI development has progressed. Because text, images, and audio are easy to digitize at enormous scale, we have built models that are exceptionally capable in those modalities. Their success then encourages us to frame more problems as text, image, or audio problems. But the physical world does not organize itself around the formats that happen to be convenient for computers.

If AI increasingly moves beyond screens and into laboratories, factories, kitchens, farms, hospitals, and other physical environments, this distinction may become more important. The question will not only be whether a model can reason about the world, but whether it can perceive enough of the world to reason well.

That is what makes sensory AI interesting to me. I initially approached the idea through cooking because it provides unusually intuitive examples of information that humans use almost without thinking. But the broader question is much larger.

We have spent a great deal of effort asking how to make machines think better. Perhaps another part of the problem is figuring out how to let them experience more of what they are trying to understand.

Maybe better AI needs better models.

But maybe, in some cases, it first needs better senses.
